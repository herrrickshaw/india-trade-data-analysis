India's Metal Import Bill Is $120 Billion. Recycling Is Already Eating Into It — Just Not Where You'd Expect.

Bare metals — gold, silver, copper, aluminium and friends — now cost India roughly $120 billion a year in imports, about 15% of everything the country buys abroad. A small group of recyclers has quietly proven that one of those import lines can be flipped into an export line. The data shows exactly which one, why it worked, and where the model goes next.

Gold alone is 9.3% of India's entire import bill — second only to crude oil. But gold is a savings phenomenon, not an industrial one. Strip out bullion and the industrial problem is copper (compounding at ~13%/yr for eight years) with an emerging aluminium-scrap dependency. That's where recycling enters.

The paradox: India imports its recycling feedstock

Hidden inside that table is the strangest pair of numbers in India's trade data: copper scrap imports of $3.4 billion and aluminium scrap imports of $4.6 billion. That is $8 billion a year of other countries' waste, bought to feed India's secondary smelters — because India's own end-of-life metal isn't collected, sorted, or channelled to authorised recyclers at anywhere near the required scale.

India doesn't lack recycling capacity. It lacks reverse logistics. The smelters are here; the scrap is imported.
The proof it can flip: lead

One metal broke the pattern. In FY2025-26, India's lead exports (chapter 78, $1.27 bn) exceeded its lead imports ($1.17 bn). India is now a net lead exporter — the only base metal where the circular loop has fully closed. Why lead? Because lead-acid batteries are the one waste stream in India with mature, price-discovered reverse logistics: every battery shop, every UPS replacement, every truck depot feeds an established collection chain back to the smelters.

Adjacent listed plays: Eco Recycling (e-waste, 64% operating margins on an asset-light services model) and Ganesha Ecosphere (PET). The battery/e-waste layer — Lohum (~70% Li-ion share), Attero, BatX, Metastable, RecycleKaro — remains private.

Three things jump out:

1. Every profitable recycler at scale is lead-first. Jain, Gravita, Pondy, Nile, Ardee — all built on the one metal where collection already works. The trade data and the corporate landscape tell the same story from opposite ends.

2. This is a spread business, not a margin business. Operating margins are 3–10%; returns come from asset turnover (Jain earns a 29% ROE on a 3.4% margin; Pondy and Nile ~19%). The dominant cost is buying scrap — which is exactly what CEEW found kills solar-panel recycling economics too (procurement is 56–68% of recurring cost). Feedstock access is the moat.

3. The models are validated; the prices aren't cheap. P/E of 31–53 and P/B above 5 (except Nile at 1.9) means the market has already paid for the circular-economy tailwind. The thesis being right and the stocks being buys are different claims.

The growth runway is that $8 billion, not new technology

Two fresh policy hooks aim squarely at the feedstock problem:

Ministry of Mines — ₹1,500 crore recycling incentive under the National Critical Mineral Mission: subsidises recovery of critical minerals from e-waste, lithium-ion battery waste, and end-of-life vehicles. E-waste and ELVs are copper- and aluminium-dense — every tonne collected domestically displaces a dollar of that $8 bn scrap import line this year.

DST — Call for Proposals on end-of-life solar PV recycling: academia–industry partnerships to develop economically viable recycling processes and equipment — targeting precisely the technology gap that makes PV recycling loss-making today.

And the next lead? Watch silver and solar

Silver imports tripled to $12.1 bn this year — partly price, partly solar-cell paste demand. CEEW's November 2025 study shows solar-module recycling currently loses ₹10,000–12,000 per tonne (feedstock cost, again). But two things flip it: EPR certificate trading extended to copper/silver/silicon (a ₹24,659/tonne swing, per CEEW), and the arrival of high-silver TOPCon modules (20–26 mg/W vs 6–10 today) in the waste stream after 2028, which turns chemical recycling profitable without subsidy. India faces 11,221 kilotonnes of cumulative PV waste by 2047 — CEEW estimates recycling it could meet 60% of the solar sector's silicon demand, 50% of aluminium, 44% of copper and 38% of silver by then. Today's loss-making niche is positioned to become the 2030s' version of the lead loop.

The takeaway

Recycling won't dent the gold bill — that's a savings-behaviour and duty-arbitrage story. But for industrial metals the sequencing is clear: lead proved the model → the ₹1,500 crore NCMM scheme and EPR rules extend it to copper and aluminium (substituting $8 bn of imported scrap first) → solar waste brings silver and silicon into the loop after 2028. The companies are already built. What the trade data says they need isn't smelters — it's India's own scrap.
